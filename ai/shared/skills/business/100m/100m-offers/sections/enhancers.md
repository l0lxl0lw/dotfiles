# Step 5: Scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees, naming

The stack from Step 4 is the offer. These five multiply it. Apply them in this order —
each one assumes the previous is in place.

**Rule for all five: never fake one.** Fake scarcity, fake deadlines, and fake bonus
values are detectable, and getting caught costs more than the conversion lift was worth.
If the user wants a countdown timer that resets, say no and explain why.

---

## 1. Scarcity — limited *supply*

Scarcity is about how much exists. It works because supply constraints signal demand, and
because the fear of loss outweighs the desire for gain.

| Type | What it looks like | When it's honest |
|------|--------------------|------------------|
| **Limited slots** | "I take 6 clients per month" | The user genuinely can't deliver more. Ask their real capacity. |
| **Limited cohort size** | "20 seats per cohort" | Group dynamics or support ratios actually degrade past the number. |
| **Limited bonuses** | "First 10 get the audit" | The bonus itself costs the user time to deliver. |
| **Never again** | "This version won't be offered after we launch v2" | They actually intend to retire it. |

**How to ask:** *"How many of these can you deliver well in a month before quality drops?"*
That number is the honest scarcity, and it's usually far lower than they've been implying.
Publishing it costs nothing and is true.

**The strongest form is a waitlist.** Selling out and making people wait is the highest
form of demand signal you can send, and it changes how the next cohort behaves before
they've paid a cent.

---

## 2. Urgency — limited *time*

Urgency is about when the window closes. Scarcity is supply, urgency is deadline; use both
and they compound.

| Type | Mechanic | Best for |
|------|----------|----------|
| **Rolling cohorts** | Enrolment closes Friday, group starts Monday | Anything delivered in groups |
| **Rolling seasonal** | "Q1 pricing ends March 31" | Services, consulting, recurring programs |
| **Pricing / bonus deadline** | Price rises or a bonus disappears at a date | Anything — the easiest honest deadline to create |
| **Exploding opportunity** | The circumstance itself expires (tax deadline, a rule change, a seasonal peak) | Rare, and by far the most powerful when real |

**Rolling cohorts are the default recommendation** for a service business with no natural
deadline: they manufacture a real, repeatable, non-fake deadline every single week, and
they improve delivery at the same time.

**How to ask:** *"Is there anything in your customer's calendar that makes this urgent
whether or not you exist?"* If yes, that's an exploding opportunity — use it and skip the
manufactured ones.

---

## 3. Bonuses — add value instead of cutting price

**The core trade: a $500 discount costs $500. A bonus with a $500 anchored value can cost
$20 to deliver.** Whenever the user reaches for a discount, reach for a bonus instead and
say why out loud.

Rules for bonuses that actually work:

- **Each bonus solves the *next* objection.** Go back to the problems list from Step 4b —
  the ones that got trimmed are your bonus candidates. A bonus that solves a problem the
  buyer doesn't have adds nothing.
- **Name it as a benefit, not a format.** "The 15-Minute Weekly Review Template" beats
  "bonus worksheet."
- **Price each one individually and say why it's worth that.** An unanchored bonus reads
  as filler.
- **Present them one at a time**, after the main offer, not as a bundle.
- **Tools and templates outperform more information.** The buyer already has too much
  information; give them the thing that removes work.
- **Highest-leverage bonuses are the ones with near-zero marginal cost and high perceived
  value** — recordings, templates, checklists, software access, a private community, a
  scorecard, done-for-you assets.

Ask the user: *"What's something you already have — a spreadsheet, a checklist, a process
doc — that took you years to build and takes you zero minutes to hand over?"* That's
usually the best bonus in the business, sitting unused.

## 4. Guarantees — reverse the risk

The single strongest lever on **perceived likelihood of achievement**, which is the most
common Value Equation constraint. The buyer's real question is "what happens to me if this
doesn't work?" A guarantee answers it.

**Before recommending any guarantee, ask two questions:**
1. *"What's your current refund rate?"*
2. *"If 10% of buyers invoked this tomorrow, does the business survive?"*

A guarantee the user can't honour is worse than none. Record both answers in the doc.

### Unconditional
"30 days, no questions asked." Highest conversion lift, highest refund exposure. Best
when the product delivers value fast and the user's delivery is genuinely reliable.

### Conditional — the default recommendation
"Do X, Y, Z and if you don't get the result, we do A." Filters out the buyers who were
never going to do the work, which improves both refund rate *and* client outcomes. Named
variants worth offering:

| Variant | The promise |
|---------|-------------|
| **Keep working free** | If you don't hit the result by the deadline, we keep working until you do, unpaid |
| **Refund + you keep everything** | Full refund, and the deliverables stay yours |
| **Pay-for-outcome credit** | Missed result converts to credit against the next engagement |
| **We pay a penalty** | Miss the target and the user pays *them* a stated amount |
| **Cover the switching cost** | We pay to move you back / cover the competitor's fee |
| **Delayed second payment** | Second instalment isn't due until the first milestone lands |

The conditions must be things the customer genuinely controls, and there must be few of
them. A guarantee with nine conditions reads as an escape hatch and converts worse than
no guarantee at all.

### Anti-guarantee
"All sales final — and here's why." Works for genuinely scarce, high-ticket, or
irreversible offers, and doubles as a filter: *"If you need a guarantee to decide, you're
not who this is for."* Only credible when demand visibly exceeds supply.

### Implied / performance-based
Revenue share, profit share, per-outcome pricing, ratchets, milestone bonuses. The
guarantee is structural — the user only gets paid if it works. Enormously persuasive, and
dangerous if attribution is murky or the customer controls the outcome. Ask *"can you
cleanly measure the result, and does anything outside your control affect it?"* before
recommending this.

**Give the user the exact wording.** Not "offer a conditional guarantee" — write the
sentence that goes on the page.

## 5. Name it — the MAGIC formula

An unnamed offer is a description. A named offer is a thing people can refer to, ask for,
and repeat to a colleague.

| Letter | Element | Example fragment |
|--------|---------|------------------|
| **M** | **M**agnet — a reason why | "Free" / "$1" / "Spring" / "New Owner" |
| **A** | **A**vatar — who exactly | "for HVAC Owner-Operators" |
| **G** | **G**oal — the dream outcome | "Book 30 Jobs" |
| **I** | **I**nterval — the time frame | "in 30 Days" |
| **C** | **C**ontainer — the word that makes it a thing | Challenge, Bootcamp, Intensive, Blueprint, Program, Accelerator, Masterclass, Sprint, System |

Not every element is required, and word order is flexible — readability beats completeness.

```
"The 30-Day HVAC Job-Booking Sprint"
"Free Pipeline Rebuild Intensive for B2B Founders"
"The 6-Week Zero-to-First-Hire Blueprint"
```

**Rotate the name over time.** When response drops, changing the magnet and the interval
resets the offer's novelty without changing what's delivered — this is why the same
program can run as a "Spring Challenge" and later a "New Year Intensive."

Generate three candidate names and let the user pick. Put the chosen one at the top of the
doc.
