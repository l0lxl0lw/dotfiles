# Steps 3–4: Score the Value Equation, then build the offer

## Step 3: The Value Equation

```
                Dream Outcome  ×  Perceived Likelihood of Achievement
    Value  =  ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
                    Time Delay  ×  Effort & Sacrifice
```

Two terms to maximise, two to minimise. Every offer that fails to convert is failing at
one specific term — and **it is almost never the one the founder thinks.** Founders
reflexively work on the dream outcome (bigger promise) when the real constraint is
perceived likelihood (nobody believes it) or effort (it looks like a second job).

Score all four with the user, 1–10, one question each. Make *them* say the number, then
argue with it.

### Dream Outcome — what they actually want

Ask: *"Finish this sentence as your customer: 'I want ______ so that ______.' Then do it
again on the second half."* Keep going until you hit something emotional or status-shaped.

The dream outcome is rarely the deliverable. Nobody wants a bookkeeping service; they want
to stop lying awake about the IRS. Nobody wants a workout program; they want to be
undeniable at the reunion. **Score it low if the user describes a feature or a process
rather than an end state their customer would say out loud.**

You mostly can't increase this term — the customer's desire is what it is. What you can do
is stop under-describing it. Most offers score 6–8 here and it is rarely the constraint.

### Perceived Likelihood of Achievement — do they believe it will work *for them*

Ask: *"On a call, when someone hesitates, what is the actual sentence they say?"*

Listen for "I've tried something like this before," "our situation is different," "how do
I know you can do this for someone like me." All three are likelihood problems.

**This is the most common real constraint and the most fixable.** Levers, in order of
power:
1. **Guarantee** — moves risk from them to you. Covered in `enhancers.md`.
2. **Proof at their exact size and shape** — three case studies from businesses that look
   like theirs beats thirty from businesses that don't.
3. **Doing more of the work yourself** — DFY beats DWY beats DIY on this term, at the cost
   of margin and scale. Name that trade explicitly.
4. **Specificity of the mechanism** — a named, explained process ("the 4-week Pipeline
   Rebuild") reads as more likely to work than "we'll help with sales."

### Time Delay — how long until they feel something

Ask: *"From the moment they pay, how long until they experience the first good thing? And
how long until the full result?"*

Two distinct numbers. The first matters far more. A twelve-month transformation with a
win on day two converts; a twelve-month transformation with a win in month four does not.

Levers: front-load a fast win, ship an onboarding artifact within 24 hours, break the
outcome into visible milestones, report progress weekly whether or not the customer asks.
If there is genuinely nothing good until month three, **invent something legitimately
valuable for week one** — an audit, a plan, a scorecard. That is not theatre if it's real.

### Effort & Sacrifice — what it costs them beyond money

Ask: *"What does the customer have to do, learn, give up, or stop doing to get this
result?"*

Include the invisible costs: the learning curve, the political cost of championing it
internally, the identity cost of admitting they needed help, the thing they must stop
doing to make room.

Levers: remove steps, do it for them, provide templates instead of instructions,
handle the migration, absorb the setup. Every step you delete raises value without
touching price.

### Naming the constraint

The constraint is the term whose improvement would move total value most — usually the
lowest-scored numerator term or the highest-scored denominator term. **Name exactly one.**
Then state the specific change that moves it and what number should respond:

> *"Constraint is perceived likelihood, 4/10. They don't doubt you can do it — they doubt
> it works at their size. Fix: a conditional guarantee plus three case studies from 5–15
> person firms. The number to watch is close rate on the second call."*

If the user disagrees with your pick, ask which of the four they'd bet on and why. If
their reasoning is evidence-based, take it. If it's "I just think our messaging is off,"
that's the dream-outcome reflex — say so.

## Step 4: Build the offer

Four moves, in order. Do not let the user jump to move 4.

### 4a. Start from the dream outcome

One sentence, in the customer's words, from Step 3. Everything below hangs off it.

### 4b. List every problem

Ask: *"Walk me through everything that goes wrong between them wanting this and them
having it."* Then push for more — you want **at least ten**, ideally more.

Two prompts that reliably surface the missing ones:
- Walk the steps in sequence. For each step, ask what goes wrong *before* it, *during*
  it, and *after* it.
- Ask about each dimension separately: what does it cost them in **money**, in **time**,
  in **effort**, and in **emotion/status**? Founders list logistics and forget that
  "looking incompetent in front of my boss" is a problem the offer can solve.

Write them down as the customer would say them, not as the business would categorise
them.

### 4c. Invert each problem into a solution

Mechanical: every problem becomes a solution named as its removal. The naming convention
is *"How to [get the outcome] without [the problem]"* — this is also where sales copy
comes from later.

```
Problem: "I don't know which leads to call first"
      -> Solution: "The Daily Priority List — who to call, in order, before 9am"

Problem: "My team won't actually use a new system"
      -> Solution: "Done-for-you rollout — we train the team and run week one"

Problem: "I'll look stupid if this fails publicly"
      -> Solution: "Private pilot with one rep before anyone else sees it"
```

Do this for every problem. It is fine — expected, even — to end with twenty solutions.
Trimming comes next.

### 4d. Pick delivery vehicles, then trim and stack

Each solution can be delivered in many ways. Vary these dimensions:

- **Group size** — one-on-one, small group, one-to-many, recorded
- **Level of done-ness** — DIY (they do it), DWY (you coach), DFY (you do it)
- **Support channel and speed** — 24/7 phone, business-hours Slack, weekly call, email
- **Format** — live, recorded, template, software, physical, in-person
- **Duration and frequency** — one-off, weekly, on-demand

Two prompts that break founders out of the obvious answer:
- *"If someone paid you 10× your price, what would you deliver?"* → surfaces the premium
  components and the real differentiators.
- *"If someone paid you 1/10th, what's the least you could do that still works?"* →
  surfaces what's actually load-bearing, and often the entire wedge offer.

**Then trim and stack.** Sort every component into the cost/value grid:

|                   | **Low cost to deliver** | **High cost to deliver** |
|-------------------|-------------------------|--------------------------|
| **High value**    | **Keep — all of it.** This is the core of the offer. | **Keep sparingly.** One or two, as the differentiator. Price for it. |
| **Low value**     | Cut. Clutter dilutes. | **Cut first, always.** This is the component quietly eating the business. |

"Cost" is the user's time and money, so you need their delivery hours from Step 2. If they
don't know, estimate together and mark it as an open number.

The output of this step is the **stack**: the surviving components, each with an anchored
standalone dollar value, totalled, sitting next to the actual price. The gap between the
total and the price *is* the offer.

```
The Pipeline Rebuild                          value
  4-week done-with-you implementation        $8,000
  The Daily Priority List system             $2,000
  Team rollout + week-one management         $4,000
  90 days of business-hours Slack access     $3,000
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Total value                               $17,000
  Your investment                            $4,500
```

Anchor each value against something real — what that component costs elsewhere, or what
the hours would cost at market rate. Invented anchors are obvious and destroy trust; if
you can't justify a number, use a smaller one.
