# Steps 1–2: The market gate, then the price posture

## Step 1: Is this a starving crowd?

> "You can't sell hot dogs to people who aren't hungry, no matter how good the hot dog."

An offer cannot rescue a bad market. Run this gate **before** building anything, and say
the verdict out loud even when it's unwelcome.

Four criteria. Ask about each; score pass / weak / fail with one line of evidence.

| # | Criterion | The question to ask | Fails when |
|---|-----------|--------------------|------------|
| 1 | **Massive pain** | "What does this problem cost them if they do nothing for another year — in dollars, hours, or consequences to them personally?" | The honest answer is "it's a bit annoying." Nice-to-haves don't get budget. |
| 2 | **Purchasing power** | "Do the people with this problem control money they can spend on it without asking anyone?" | The sufferer and the budget holder are different people, or the budget is zero. |
| 3 | **Easy to target** | "Where do 1,000 of them already gather — a list, a subreddit, a conference, an association, an ad platform audience?" | "They're everywhere" — which means nowhere you can reach cheaply. |
| 4 | **Growing** | "Is this group getting bigger or smaller over the next three years, and why?" | Shrinking market. You'll fight for a smaller pie every year. |

**How to push.** "Small business owners" is not a market. "Owner-operators of 2–8 truck
HVAC companies doing $1–3M who just lost their office manager" is a market. Push until
you get a sentence you could turn into an ad-targeting spec.

**If the market fails.** Say it plainly, once, without softening: *"The offer isn't the
problem. Criterion 2 fails — the people in pain don't hold the budget. Any offer we build
lands on someone who has to go ask permission, and that's why your close rate is what it
is."* Then give them the choice: change the market, change the buyer, or proceed knowing
the ceiling. If they proceed, build the offer anyway and record the failed criterion at
the top of the doc.

**Niching up the price.** The same service, aimed at a narrower and more specific
customer, sells for multiples more. The sequence to walk them through:

```
"time management course"                     $19
"time management for sales professionals"     $99
"time management for outbound SDRs"          $499
"time management for outbound SDRs at
 Series-B SaaS companies"                  $1,999
```

Nothing changed but the specificity of who it's for. If the user resists narrowing
because "we'd be leaving money on the table," name the pattern: broad positioning is what
makes them a commodity, and commodities compete on price.

## Step 2: Price posture — commodity or Grand Slam

**The definition of a commodity:** a product available from many sources, therefore
compared on price. If a prospect can put your offer beside two others in a spreadsheet
and pick the cheapest, you are a commodity, and your margin will get competed to zero
regardless of how good your work is.

The escape is not "be better." It's to make comparison structurally impossible — a
different combination of deliverables, guarantee, and terms than anyone else is selling.

**The virtuous cycle of price.** This is the argument to make when the user is scared to
raise their price. Higher price → more margin → you can afford better delivery, better
support, better people, better acquisition → clients get better results → they value it
more and refer more → you can charge more. The vicious cycle runs the other way: cut
price → thin margin → cheap delivery → bad results → clients churn and complain →
you must discount to fill the pipeline → thinner margin.

There is a second effect that matters more than the money: **people value what they pay
for.** Clients who pay premium prices show up, do the work, and get results. Cheap clients
ghost. So price is not just how you capture value — it's part of how you *deliver* it.
A price too low actively harms your outcomes.

**Price to value, never to cost.** The question is never "what did this cost me to make"
or "what do competitors charge." It's "what is the outcome worth to this buyer?" Ask
directly: *"If this works exactly as promised, what is that worth to them in the first
year — in revenue gained, cost avoided, or hours returned?"* You want a number. Then the
price conversation is about a fraction of that number, and the anchoring is done.

**The questions for this step:**

1. "What do you charge now, and how did you land on that number?" — *Listen for cost-plus
   or competitor-matching. Both are commodity behaviour. Name it.*
2. "If this works, what is it worth to the buyer in year one?" — *Push for a figure. This
   becomes the anchor for the whole offer.*
3. "What's your gross margin at that price, and how many hours does delivery take you?"
   — *You need this for trim & stack later. Missing numbers go in the doc's open list.*

**What to do with the answers.** State a position: *"You're charging $2,000 for something
worth $40,000 to them in year one. That's not humility, it's a signal — at 5% of the
value you look like a tool, not a solution. The Grand Slam version of this is $8,000–
$12,000 with a guarantee that makes the risk theirs to lose, not yours."* Then say what
evidence would change your mind — usually: their close rate at the current price. If they
close 90% at $2,000, they were always too cheap. If they close 20%, price isn't the
constraint and the Value Equation will tell you what is.
