# Steps 3–4: Commit to volume, then scale

## Step 3: The Rule of 100

The one commitment that makes the channel work. **100 units of advertising a day, every
weekday, for 100 days**, before judging whether it worked.

| Channel | The daily 100 |
|---------|---------------|
| Warm outreach | 100 people contacted |
| Content | 100 minutes spent making and posting |
| Cold outreach | 100 people contacted |
| Paid ads | $100 spent |

The point is not the specific number. The point is **a fixed daily quantity, decided in
advance, that isn't renegotiated on bad days.** Almost every "marketing doesn't work for
my business" is a volume story: someone sent 60 emails over three weeks and concluded cold
email is dead.

**Adapt the number honestly.** For a solo founder with a day job, 100 personalised cold
emails is not real and pretending otherwise guarantees failure on day four. Negotiate down
to something they will genuinely do — 25/day — and then hold that number as fixed. **A
smaller number they hit beats a bigger number they abandon.** What must not move is the
commitment to a number and a review date.

**Get all three in writing:**

```
Volume:  50 contacts/day, Mon–Fri
Start:   2026-08-04
Review:  after 1,000 contacts, not before  ← this line is the load-bearing one
```

The review threshold matters as much as the volume. Without it, the user evaluates on day
six, sees noise, and switches channels. Say explicitly: *"You are not allowed to conclude
anything about this channel before 1,000 contacts. Not 'it's not working,' not 'the list
is wrong.' Just keep sending."*

**The honest expectation to set:**

| Channel | Time to first real signal |
|---------|--------------------------|
| Warm outreach | Days |
| Cold outreach | 2–4 weeks at volume |
| Paid ads | 2–6 weeks, and it costs money to find out |
| Content | 3–6 months |

Say this before they commit, not after. A user who chose content expecting results in
three weeks will quit in week four, and it will look like the advice was wrong.

## Step 4: Scaling — More, Better, New

When one channel works, the growth options are exactly three, and they go in this order.

**1. More.** Same channel, more volume. Almost always the right answer and almost never
the one people pick, because it's boring. If cold email at 50/day produces 5 calls/week,
150/day produces 15. Exhaust this before anything else. Ask: *"What stops you doing 3× the
volume — time, list size, or delivery capacity?"* The answer is the real constraint and
usually solvable with a hire or a tool.

**2. Better.** Same channel, same volume, higher conversion. Improve the hook, the list,
the offer, the follow-up. Only meaningful once volume is high enough for the data to be
real — under a thousand attempts, you are optimising noise.

**3. New.** Add a channel. **Last, and only when the first is genuinely maxed.** Adding a
channel early is the failure pattern the whole skill exists to prevent, and it always
feels like progress because it's new.

When a user asks about a second channel, the question back is: *"Are you at the maximum
volume you can physically do on the first one?"* If no, the answer is More.

## The Lead Getters — other people advertising for you

Scaling past your own hours means other people generating leads on your behalf. Four
sources, in rough order of how early they're available:

### 1. Customers — referrals
Cheapest and highest-converting leads that exist. Almost nobody asks systematically.

- **Ask at the moment of peak happiness**, not at renewal. Build the ask into delivery at
  the point where the customer just got a result.
- **Make it specific.** "Who do you know who's dealing with the same thing you were six
  months ago?" beats "know anyone who'd benefit?"
- **Give them something to give.** A referral is easier when it's an introduction to
  something free, not a sales pitch.
- **Incentivise both sides.** Something for the referrer and something for the referred.
- Ask: *"What's your current referral rate, and do you ever actually ask?"* The answer is
  usually "no" and this is often the single fastest fix in the entire session.

### 2. Employees
Hire people to do the outreach. Only sensible once the process works and is documented —
you cannot delegate a channel you haven't personally made work. Ask: *"Could you write
down exactly what you do each day well enough that someone else could do it?"* If not,
they're not ready to hire for it.

### 3. Agencies
Rent expertise and speed. Fastest to start, most expensive, and you don't own the skill
afterward. The honest use: hire an agency to get a channel working, insist on learning how
while they do it, then bring it in-house. Warn about the standard failure — handing over a
channel you've never run yourself means you can't tell competence from noise.

### 4. Affiliates
People who sell your thing for a cut. Highest leverage, hardest to start, because you need
a proven offer with a known conversion rate before anyone will promote it. Requires
tracking, a payout structure, and material for them to use.

**Where to point the user.** Referrals are available to every business today and are
almost always the untapped one. The other three are for later, and saying "later"
explicitly is more useful than describing them in detail.
