# Step 3: The four stages and their plays

A complete money model has four stages. Diagnose which one is missing or weak, then pick
**one or two plays** from that stage. Not the whole menu — one change shipped beats four
designed.

| Stage | Job | Missing when |
|-------|-----|--------------|
| **Attraction** | Turn a stranger into a paying customer at low friction, ideally breaking even on acquisition | CAC is high and there's nothing cheap to say yes to |
| **Upsell** | Take the maximum profit inside the first 30 days from someone who already trusts you | Average order value equals the list price; nothing is offered after checkout |
| **Downsell** | Convert the no's into smaller yeses | Rejection means the lead is gone forever |
| **Continuity** | Make the revenue recur so it compounds | Every month starts from zero |

**How to diagnose.** One question per stage, asked in order, stopping at the first clear
gap:

1. *"What's the cheapest, lowest-risk thing someone can buy from you today?"* — no answer,
   or "the main thing," means attraction is missing.
2. *"What do you offer immediately after someone buys?"* — "nothing" means upsell is
   missing. This is the most common gap and usually the fastest fix.
3. *"What happens when someone says no to the price?"* — "I follow up in a few months"
   means downsell is missing.
4. *"Does anyone pay you every month?"* — "no" means continuity is missing, but check it's
   *appropriate* before recommending it.

---

## Attraction offers

Getting the first yes. The goal is not profit on this transaction — it's a customer,
acquired at or near break-even, who can be sold to properly afterward.

| Play | Mechanic | Works when |
|------|----------|------------|
| **Win your money back** | Buy in; hit a defined milestone and it's refunded or credited | You want compliance as well as a sale — the condition makes customers succeed |
| **Giveaway** | A high-value prize draw; everyone who enters gets a substantial "partial scholarship" or credit toward the paid thing | You need volume of entrants and have something worth winning |
| **Decoy** | Advertise the basic version; most buyers move themselves up to the premium once they see both | The premium is genuinely and visibly better |
| **Free with a catch** | Product free, but they pay shipping, setup, or a deposit | Marginal cost is low and the follow-on offer is real |
| **Buy X get Y free** | Preserves the headline price while raising perceived value; better than discounting because the price anchor survives | Y costs you far less than its perceived value |
| **Pay less now or more later** | Small amount today, remainder on a milestone or a date | Reduces the entry decision without giving away margin |

**The rule for all of them:** an attraction offer without something behind it is just a
discount, and it will make the numbers worse, not better. **Never let the user ship one of
these before the upsell that pays for it exists.** Say this explicitly — it is the way this
stage most often backfires.

---

## Upsell offers

Where the money actually is. A customer who just bought is more likely to buy again *right
now* than at any other moment they will ever be in. Most businesses waste that moment
completely.

| Play | Mechanic | Works when |
|------|----------|------------|
| **Classic upsell** | Immediately after purchase, offer the bigger version or the accelerator | Always. This is the default and the first thing to add. |
| **Anchor upsell** | Present the expensive option first; everything after it reads as reasonable | There's a genuine premium tier |
| **Menu upsell** | Diagnose first, then prescribe an exact set — "you don't need A or B, you need C and D" | Consultative sale. Removing options builds more trust than adding them. |
| **Rollover** | The amount already paid credits toward a larger commitment | You have a small entry product and a large main one |
| **A/B upsell** | Ask *which*, never *whether* — "the 6-week or the 12-week?" | Live or conversational selling |
| **Event upsell** | Sell the next thing at the moment of a delivered result | You have milestones the customer visibly reaches |

**The timing rule: immediately.** Not in the follow-up email, not next month. The offer
should arrive while the decision to trust you is still warm. Ask the user *"what happens
in the 60 seconds after someone pays?"* — the answer is usually "a receipt," and that's
the opportunity.

**The unselling move** (the heart of the menu upsell) is worth teaching explicitly:
publicly talk them *out* of something they don't need before prescribing what they do.
It costs a little revenue and buys enormous credibility, and it usually nets higher.

---

## Downsell offers

Rejections are not dead — they're a smaller yes waiting for a smaller ask. Most businesses
throw away every no.

| Play | Mechanic | Works when |
|------|----------|------------|
| **Payment plan** | Same offer, split over time. Total often *higher*. | The objection is cash flow, not value — ask which it is |
| **Free trial with penalty** | Start free; a fee applies if they violate agreed terms (no-show, cancel late, don't do the work) | You need commitment without an up-front barrier |
| **Feature downsell** | Remove components to reach a lower price — and the removal itself demonstrates what they were getting | The offer is genuinely modular |
| **Re-upsell** | After they take the stripped version, the missing pieces become the natural next purchase | The removed components are things they'll miss |
| **Seesaw** | Ask their available budget, then build the offer that fits it | High-touch service sale, and delivery scope can genuinely flex |
| **Service downgrade** | Same outcome, lower touch — group instead of one-on-one, DIY instead of DFY | Delivery has multiple honest intensity levels |

**The diagnostic question before choosing:** *"When someone says no, is it 'I don't have
the money' or 'I don't think it's worth it'?"* Only the first is a downsell. The second is
an offer problem — send them to `/100m-offers`, because a cheaper version of something
nobody wants sells no better.

---

## Continuity offers

Recurring revenue. It compounds, which is why it's stage four rather than stage one — it's
the most valuable and the most often bolted onto something that can't support it.

| Play | Mechanic | Works when |
|------|----------|------------|
| **Continuity bonus** | A high-value one-off is included free *with* the subscription | You have a valuable asset costing nothing to duplicate |
| **Waived fee** | Setup or onboarding fee disappears in exchange for a longer commitment | There's a real setup cost to waive |
| **Prepay discount** | Two months free for annual up front | Cash today matters more than total revenue — often the fastest way to pass Gate 1 |
| **Free trial into paid** | Card captured up front, billing starts after the trial | Value is obvious quickly and churn after trial is low |
| **Usage-based** | Charges scale with consumption | Value genuinely tracks usage |
| **28-day billing** | Bill every 28 days rather than monthly — 13 cycles a year instead of 12 | Only with clear, prominent disclosure. See the warning below. |

**Before recommending any of these, ask: *"What does the customer actually receive in
month two?"*** If the answer is vague, continuity is a churn and chargeback machine and
you should say no. Recurring billing has to be backed by recurring value.

**On 28-day billing:** it does raise annual revenue by roughly 8%, and it is only
acceptable with plain, prominent disclosure at the point of sale. Present it as a
disclosure-dependent option, never as a trick, and say so directly. If the user's instinct
is to bury it, that's the signal not to do it.

**On prepay:** frequently the single highest-leverage change available to a business
failing Gate 1, because it converts twelve months of slow collection into cash today
without changing the product at all. Check it before anything more elaborate.
