# Verticals — and where his heuristics break

Read the relevant part in Phase 0. This matters more than it looks: several
heuristics **invert** between verticals, and a few are simply wrong outside the
kinds of business the source material is drawn from.

## What the corpus is actually made of

The sessions skew heavily toward owner-operated businesses with real marginal cost
per unit delivered: home services, clinics and med spas, gyms and studios,
construction trades, agencies, coaching and info products, and local
multi-location operators. Revenue is typically single-digit millions with the
founder still in the building.

That's the calibration set. Advice is reliable inside it and needs translation
outside it.

## Services / agency / consulting

Closest fit — almost no translation needed.

- Capacity is people-hours, so the fully-booked-and-broke playbook applies almost
  verbatim.
- Selling hours or retainers is the commodity trap in its purest form; price to
  outcome.
- The founder-as-best-salesperson point holds strongly.
- Talent walking off with clients is the central risk; the career-path and
  loyalty-structure playbook is the important one.

## Local / physical / multi-location

His home turf; the most specific material lives here.

- Speed to lead is decisive on search-intent traffic.
- Memberships do double duty: retention plus schedulable demand, which raises
  utilization without hiring.
- Measure per constraining unit — per chair, room, technician, square foot.
- The expansion litmus test and the pre-lease market test apply directly, and the
  market test is the single most under-used lever in the vertical.
- Be skeptical of franchising; make them do the unit arithmetic.

## Software / SaaS / dev tools

**This is where he breaks.** Keep the diagnostic method; discard several
prescriptions. Be explicit with the user about which is which — encoding him
uncritically here produces confidently wrong advice.

### What still holds, and holds strongly

- **Constraint thinking.** One rate limiter, work it to completion. Fully general.
- **Numbers discipline.** Refusing to advise without figures is if anything more
  important where dashboards create an illusion of measurement.
- **Exhaust before expand.** The urge to add a second product instead of finishing
  the first is if anything worse in software.
- **Retention outranks acquisition.** Maps cleanly to net revenue retention, and
  the leaky-bucket logic is exact.
- **Volume and under-testing.** "We tried content" meaning eleven posts is the same
  failure in any vertical.
- **New process, old person.** Holds for onboarding, support, and sales hires.
- **You are the bottleneck.** Founder-led sales that never gets systematized is the
  standard software version.

### What inverts or fails

- **Marginal cost is near zero**, so the capacity story changes shape. "Fully
  booked and thin margin" usually isn't delivery hours — it's engineering
  throughput, support load, or infrastructure cost per account. Don't reach for
  the repricing-for-utilization playbook without checking which one it is.
  Utilization-per-technician has no clean analogue.
- **Paid acquisition often doesn't work**, particularly for developer tools.
  Developers block ads and distrust them, buy bottom-up, and evaluate by trying.
  His Core Four maps to documentation, open source, technical content, community,
  and developer relations. Prescribing "spend more on ads" here is straightforwardly
  bad advice for a large class of software businesses.
- **The buyer is often not the payer.** Bottom-up adoption means the person who
  chooses the tool and the person who signs the invoice differ, which breaks the
  single-decision-maker sales motion the playbooks assume.
- **Price is a strategic position, not a lever to yank.** Raising price is far more
  reversible in a service business than in one with published pricing, a
  self-serve funnel, and competitors one tab away.
- **Distribution can be a genuine constraint that volume cannot fix.** Platform
  dependencies, procurement cycles, security review, and integration gates are
  real structural limits. "What stops you from 20x" sometimes has an honest answer
  that isn't a motivation problem.
- **The founder-must-sell point weakens.** Founder-led selling is still valuable
  early, but for genuinely self-serve products the equivalent skill is owning
  positioning and onboarding, not being on calls.

### How to run a software session

Use Phases 0-3 unchanged — the intake, premise challenge, inflation move, and
number verification all transfer. At Phase 4, translate before prescribing, and
say you're translating.

## Domain-agnostic default

If they won't name a vertical, ask two questions that recover what actually
matters:

1. **"What's your marginal cost to serve one more customer?"** Near zero puts you
   in software-like territory; meaningful cost puts you in services-like territory.
2. **"Does one person's time cap how much you can deliver?"** Yes means the
   capacity playbooks apply as written.

Those two answers determine which prescriptions are safe far better than the
industry label does.

## The general caveats

Carry these regardless of vertical.

**The advice assumes a working offer and existing revenue.** The whole method is
built for businesses with something that already works, being asked to do more of
it. Applied pre-product-market-fit, "increase volume" scales a bad offer and
"exhaust the current channel" entrenches something that should be abandoned. If
they're pre-revenue with no customers, this is the wrong room — send them to gstack
`/office-hours`.

**Survivorship runs through all of it.** The prescriptions come from businesses
that made it, and the confident causal claims about what did it are unfalsifiable
from inside the sample.

**Speed of diagnosis is a feature of the format, not a virtue.** These are
five-to-fifteen-minute answers on a stage with a room watching. That format rewards
decisiveness and punishes "I'd want to see your numbers first." You're not on a
stage. Keep the directness; buy the extra question.

**Blunt is not the same as right.** Confidence is a rhetorical style. When the
call is irreversible — selling, firing, debt, relocating, betting the company —
state your actual confidence, name what you'd want to know, and let them decide
with clear eyes.
