# Playbooks — the ordered fix for each constraint

Read this in Phase 4, after the constraint is named. Every playbook is a
**sequence**. Give it in order and say which step is first.

Where a playbook's territory belongs to a sibling skill, it says so — hand off
rather than half-doing it.

## The master ordering

**Offer → sales motion → traffic.**

Fix in that direction always. Traffic pointed at a broken offer costs more per
customer and teaches you nothing. Most owners want to start at traffic because
it's the step you can buy.

## Volume: the reason most prescriptions fail

Vague prescriptions get ignored, and under-volume is the most common reason
something "didn't work." Attach a number they can count.

The shape of the ask, using paid creative as the example: you don't write ads, you
write **hooks** against a small number of core message bodies. Fifty hooks over
three to five bodies yields a large ad set from a single recording session, because
the hook is what's actually being tested. Hooks port across industries — the same
opening line can work in unrelated businesses — so studying what keeps running in
your own feed is a legitimate research method.

When someone says they're producing five or six creatives a week at meaningful
spend, the honest answer is that it's off by roughly an order of magnitude. Give
the specific weekly count you want and let them push back on it.

The general principle: whatever volume they're at, find out whether the channel has
ever been tested *at volume*. It usually hasn't, and "the channel doesn't work" is
almost always "we did it a few times."

Depth on channels and volume rules: → `/100m-leads`.

## Playbook: not enough leads

Do not run this if retention is broken — fix the leak first.

1. **Pick one channel.** Warm outreach, cold outreach, content, or paid. Say out
   loud that all of them work and the losing move is doing three badly.
2. **Match the channel to the buying moment.** Search-intent traffic *channels*
   demand that already exists: warmer, more expensive per lead, and it demands
   near-instant follow-up — minutes, not hours, because you're racing everyone
   else who bought that click. Social traffic *creates* demand in people who
   weren't looking, which is the only option when nobody wakes up wanting your
   category. It needs far more creative volume in exchange.
3. **Commit to a volume and a date**, then forbid switching channels for the
   duration.
4. **Instrument it** so the next conversation has real numbers.

→ `/100m-leads` for the full channel taxonomy and volume rules.

## Playbook: they see it and don't buy

1. Diagnose which term of the value proposition is actually broken.
2. Stop selling the unit; sell the outcome.
3. Build the sales motion if one doesn't exist. Ask them to walk you through it
   end to end — the gaps announce themselves.
4. Check for the warm-to-cold mismatch (see `constraint-tree.md`) before blaming
   the channel.

→ `/100m-offers`.

## Playbook: fully booked, thin margin

This one is this skill's territory and it's the most commonly misdiagnosed state.
They will ask for help hiring or marketing. Refuse both.

1. **Reprice and repackage to the outcome.** Sessions, hours, and seats invite
   comparison and cap you at time available.
2. **Introduce recurring/membership structure.** Improves retention *and* makes
   scheduling predictable enough to raise utilization without hiring.
3. **Raise utilization** on whatever unit actually constrains you — per
   technician, chair, room, square foot, engineer.
4. **Then backfill talent**, affordable now that margin exists.
5. **Then** consider marketing.

Say explicitly: more leads right now makes this worse.

## Playbook: talent that walks off with your customers

Common wherever the relationship is one-to-one and personal — clinics, salons,
studios, agencies, anything where a named practitioner builds the rapport. When
they leave they take a slice of the book with them.

1. **Career path first.** A defined progression is what makes staying rational.
2. **Structure loyalty to the brand, not the individual** — how work is assigned,
   how clients are introduced and rotated, whose name is on the outcome. This is
   the hard part, because clients legitimately prefer the person who knows their
   preferences.
3. **Pay more, which requires margin** — so this playbook usually runs *after* the
   margin playbook, not before. Owners try to solve it with loyalty appeals
   precisely because they can't afford the real fix yet. Name that.
4. **Consider real equity-like incentives at the unit level.** Phantom stock or
   profit share vesting over time, held by the people who actually hold the client
   relationships rather than only by managers.

## Playbook: you are the bottleneck

1. **Separate the two things owners conflate.** Not doing the craft work is not
   the same as not running the place. If decisions still escalate, they're still
   the constraint.
2. **Build the process while still in the seat, then hand it over** — new process,
   old person. Never both new at once.
3. **Roleplay the escalations before delegating.** Walk a manager through concrete
   failures — a pipe bursts, a client threatens to leave, someone no-shows — and
   the answer must never be "call you." Teach the reasoning, not the answer: what
   would *I* do in this situation? Then do that. Pair it with a hard filter on what
   genuinely warrants a call.
4. **Pay for ownership of problems**, commonly a share of the unit's profit — the
   trade being explicitly that the owner's phone stops ringing.
5. **Never delegate traffic, conversion, or delivery.** Legal, accounting,
   insurance, bookkeeping: fine.

## Playbook: should I open location two (or hire the GM, or replicate)?

A three-part litmus test, and the first part is much harder than anyone expects:

1. **The existing unit runs without you for six months.** Not "you're not on the
   floor" — you're not making decisions and problems don't escalate to you. Start
   with a week, then a month, and watch what breaks. What breaks is your process
   documentation, not your team.
2. **Profitability holds or grows across those six months.** This is what proves
   the unit is fundamentally sound rather than propped up by founder presence. A
   unit that decays the moment attention moves will decay again, and now twice.
3. **A real opening plan** that gets the new unit toward roughly half capacity
   *before* it opens. Chains have this dialed to specific actions at specific day
   counts before opening. Most independents open and hope.

Then two more things:

**Test the market before signing the lease.** The highest-return test available in
any location-based business, and almost nobody runs it: take your best-performing
ad, run it into three or four candidate areas for a small fixed budget each, and
compare lead cost. Variation of several-fold within a single city is normal. Picking
the cheap market is a larger and more durable lever than anything you'll do to the
funnel afterwards — and it costs a few thousand dollars instead of a five-year
lease. Open across town rather than adjacent, so you're not cannibalizing.

**Sequence it against cash.** Once the test passes, the only remaining question is
how much cash flow funds the next opening, and that sets the timeline.

### On franchising

Be skeptical, and make them do the arithmetic. Most people franchise for status
rather than economics. Ask how many franchised units it takes to reach their
target outcome, then how many owned units it takes to reach the same outcome. The
owned number is routinely a small fraction of the franchised number, at which
point the question answers itself. Franchising is a different business — you become
a seller of franchises, with different skills and obligations — not a scaling
tactic for the business you have.

## Playbook: which service or offer do I kill?

They're usually scared to cut the thing they started with. Before touching it:

1. **Establish what it actually feeds.** The service that looks unprofitable is
   often the entry point that qualifies you for everything else — the credential
   that wins the larger work. Cut it and the pipeline goes with it.
2. **Get the real number, not the remembered one.** Ask what share of new business
   originates there. Expect the estimate to move under questioning; when it
   drifts, say so and insist on the actual attribution. Owners are systematically
   wrong about where their business comes from.
3. **Prove the replacement first.** If something has replaced it as the lead
   source, is that replacement reliable, fast, and scalable? Only cut once the
   answer is yes on all three.
4. **Then cut decisively.** Capital-intensive, hard-to-hire-for, hard-to-fix
   service lines are a tax on attention as well as cash.

## Playbook: too many offers / businesses / directions

The reframe that does the work: **the job of the business is to satisfy customers,
not the owner's appetite for new projects.** Shiny-object switching feels like
ambition and functions like attrition.

1. **Name the real cost** — not money, attention. Two businesses at half attention
   both underperform, and it compounds through every hire in each.
2. **Pick the platform**: the one with the best returns and the clearest path.
3. **Stack onto it rather than diversifying away from it.** Bolt adjacent products
   or acquisitions onto the platform where they share customers and operations.
   Stacking compounds; diversifying divides.
4. **Set a revisit date** so it feels like sequencing rather than abandonment.

## Playbook: should I sell?

Slow down here — this is irreversible and your confidence should be stated.

1. **What do they actually want?** Ask it directly, including the version of the
   question that isn't about business. An owner who wants out of the work and an
   owner who wants a specific number need opposite advice.
2. **Establish the real multiple basis** — profit, adjusted honestly, and how much
   of it depends on them personally. Owner-dependent profit is discounted hard or
   structured into an earn-out that keeps them working anyway.
3. **Compare against the do-nothing path.** What does holding for three more years
   produce, given the growth actually achievable? Do this arithmetic explicitly.
4. **Name the readiness gaps** — owner dependency, concentration in one customer
   or channel, undocumented process — because each is both a discount at sale and
   a thing worth fixing whether or not they sell.

If the honest answer is that the business as structured will never reach the number
they want, say so plainly and immediately, then give the paths that would. That is
the most valuable thing in the session and the reason to be blunt at all.
